Does showing an AI's confidence stop people from following wrong advice? An experiment on credit decisions.

Madeleine Sieber · CAS ETH in Machine Learning in Finance and Insurance · 2026

The short answer: no, not in this study. Showing the AI's confidence as a percentage did not measurably reduce how often people followed wrong recommendations, and it did not make decisions more accurate.

Two things stood out instead. People rarely changed their mind at all. And the percentage itself turned out to be ambiguous: depending on how you read it, it either contradicted the AI or almost gave away the answer.

How the study worked

Participants judged 18 historical loan applications from the South German Credit Dataset, where we know whether each loan was repaid. For every case they first decided on their own (approve or reject), rated how sure they were, then saw an AI recommendation and made a final decision.

Half of the participants saw the recommendation alone (Group A). The other half also saw a confidence value in percent (Group B). The recommendations came from six large language models; where most of them were wrong, participants saw the wrong recommendation.

89participants, about 70 % from banking and finance
18cases: 6 easy, 6 medium, 6 hard (difficulty set in a pilot with credit analysts)
7 of 18AI recommendations were wrong (1 easy, 2 medium, 4 hard)
1,602decisions analysed, preregistered on OSF

At a glance

H1 · The Switch Supported, but fragile

We expectedOn hard cases, people switch to the AI more often than on easy ones.

We foundEasy cases: 0.2 % switched. Hard cases: 6 %.

What it meansYou can only switch when you disagree, and on hard cases people disagreed with the AI far more often.

On easy cases, people disagreed with the AI in only 9 of 534 judgements; on hard cases in 151 of 534. Once they disagreed, about one in five followed the AI, on hard cases and in both groups alike. The large effect mostly reflects how often there was a reason to switch, not how readily people did. Statistically significant (odds ratio 37.6, p = .001), but it disappears if a single easy case is left out.

H2 · The Shield Not testable

We expectedOn hard cases, people who see the % (B) switch to the AI less often.

We foundHard cases: A 5.3 % · B 6.7 % switched, the opposite direction.

What it meansThe % made nobody more careful. If anything, the opposite, but the difference is not significant.

Group B made no switch at all on easy cases, so the preregistered statistical model could not be estimated. The additional tests found no group difference (hard cases only: odds ratio 1.28, p = .51).

H3 · The Target Not supported

We expectedOn hard cases, people who see the % (B) make more correct decisions.

We foundHard cases correct: A 49 % · B 53 %.

What it meansNo better decisions. Group B was already slightly ahead before seeing the AI.

Before the recommendation, B was at 54.8 % and A at 51.9 % on hard cases, so the gap was there from the start. On hard cases, accuracy even dropped slightly after the recommendation in both groups: four of the six hard cases were AI errors, so the AI was right less often than the participants themselves (odds ratio 1.23, p = .33).

H4 · The Cap Not testable

We expectedThe % helps experts more than others.

We foundNo sign that expertise mattered.

What it meansExperts were no more accurate to begin with, and did not use the % better.

People who regularly make credit decisions were initially right 64.6 % of the time, the others 64.1 %. How expert people rated themselves went with how sure they felt, not with how often they were right.

When did people switch?

Across all cases, people changed their decision in only 4 to 5 % of trials. The table shows what happened in the situations where it mattered.

What else we found

Same hard cases

The more language models got a case wrong, the more people got it wrong too, before seeing any AI (ρ = .82). On the AI-error cases, 73 % of people were initially wrong, on the others 11 %. Most likely, some cases were simply hard for every judge, not that humans and models think alike.

Noticed, not used

Group B remembered the percentages: 29 of 40 named a displayed value exactly. The number shifted how sure people felt, but hardly what they decided.

Liked more

Group B rated the AI as more useful than Group A (4.9 vs. 3.9 on a 1–7 scale), although their decisions were no better. Descriptive, not tested.

Sure means stubborn

The surer people were of their own judgement, the less they switched, towards wrong and correct advice alike. Confidence worked as resistance to advice in general, not as a filter for bad advice.

The number was ambiguous

Group B was told that the percentage shows how often the AI was right in similar cases. But for correct reject recommendations, the values had been set as the probability of approval, so a correct recommendation could appear as Reject · 14 %.

Read as instructed, such a number says the AI is probably wrong. Read as a probability of approval, the numbers pointed to the right answer in 17 of 18 cases. Group B did not exploit this, so people either ignored the number or read it inconsistently. The study therefore tests this particular display, not confidence displays in general.

What a clearer display would need:

  • One meaning only: how likely this recommendation is correct, so never below 50 %.
  • Values from a model or a stated calibration rule, lower for errors on average but overlapping.
  • Frequencies with a word: “right in about 6 of 10 similar cases · uncertain”.
  • A comprehension check before the task, and the case kept visible next to the number.

Limits

  • Few switches, low power. Switching was far rarer than planned for, so a real but small effect could have been missed. The null results are not proof that confidence displays never work.
  • Difficulty and AI errors overlap. Hard cases had more AI errors (1, 2 and 4 per level), and medium and hard cases did not differ in practice.
  • One case shown incompletely. Case 17 lacked credit history and purpose; leaving it out changes nothing.
  • The benchmark contains chance. "Correct" means matching what actually happened to a loan from the 1970s; good borrowers sometimes default.
  • Registered after data collection. The analyses were preregistered before any results were looked at, but after the data were collected.
